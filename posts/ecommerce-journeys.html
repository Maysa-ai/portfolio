Essay: Product Discovery

What e-commerce taught me about designing complex user journeys

A journey doesn't break where the screens look dated. It breaks where the systems behind them disagree with each other.

When I joined Pepperfry to lead the pre-cart shopping experience, the brief looked like an interface problem: redesign the journey across Web, iOS and Android. It wasn't. The product had evolved into a collection of disconnected experiences — different teams, different assumptions, different priorities, stacked on top of each other over time. The organization didn't need new screens. It needed a shared understanding of what the product should become.

A journey is a system, not a funnel

Once we had a real discovery practice running, two problems surfaced — and neither was a design problem in the usual sense. Search and search results were the single biggest drop-off point in the journey: not because the results page looked bad, but because the underlying logic wasn't finding what customers actually meant. And customers were confused about what they'd actually pay — price, MRP, discount and cart-level pricing were each technically correct and collectively incomprehensible.

Neither of those breaks in a wireframe. They break in the gap between systems — search relevance logic on one side, pricing and checkout logic on the other, each built correctly in isolation and never reconciled from the customer's side of the screen. Designing a complex journey means designing where those systems meet, not just what each screen looks like.

Translation is a design discipline

Large redesigns create disagreement by default. Design sees one priority, engineering sees another, the business side has commercial objectives — everyone working hard, not always toward the same destination. Most of my actual work stopped being about managing a backlog and became about translation: turning business strategy into decisions engineering could build against, helping engineering explain technical trade-offs in terms the business side could weigh, and grounding the whole conversation in evidence rather than opinion.

On the pricing confusion specifically, that meant working directly with the Checkout team to redesign how price information was presented, and validating the change through A/B testing rather than shipping on conviction. The redesign of the screen was the easy part. Getting everyone to agree on what "clear" meant was the actual work.

Journeys have to respect what the platform can carry

The redesign wasn't happening in isolation — Pepperfry was migrating from a legacy PHP stack to Node.js and Angular at the same time. Every product decision had to be sequenced against what the platform could actually support at that point in the migration. A journey that looks right on paper but can't be built against the current technical reality isn't a design win, it's a delay waiting to happen. Good journey design includes knowing what to sequence, not just what to imagine.

I don't try to eliminate complexity. I uncover the clarity hidden within it.

The pattern, applied

The same method that shaped this redesign is the one I now apply to any complex product problem:

Build

Find where the systems disagree, not just where the screen looks wrong.

Align

Translate between teams with evidence until "clear" means the same thing to everyone.

Amplify

Sequence against what the platform can actually carry, not what looks best on paper.
