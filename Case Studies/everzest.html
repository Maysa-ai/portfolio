01 / Context

Why get closer to the build?

After leading product work across different environments, I wanted a more practical understanding of how AI systems come together—not just what they could promise.

I started with the business problem and the workflow around it, then worked backward into the technical foundations: data, models, applications, and reliability. The goal was not to become an engineer overnight. It was to make more grounded product decisions.

02 / Experiments

Two workflows. Two product questions.

I explored where AI could reduce repetitive work and help people move from raw information to a useful next step. Each prototype gave me a way to test the shape of the experience—and see what still needed to be proven.

03 / Approach

Learn by building, not by guessing.

I treated the work as a product learning loop: frame a real workflow, learn enough technology to make it tangible, then use the prototype to expose assumptions and constraints.

01

Frame the problem

Start with the business need, user journey, inputs, and the decision or handoff the workflow should support.

02

Learn the foundations

Build working knowledge across Python, data preparation, ML concepts, LLMs, embeddings, vector databases, and RAG.

03

Prototype the flow

Use Streamlit and automation tools to turn the concept into something concrete enough to inspect and test.

04

Identify the gap

Separate a working demonstration from a validated product—and name the evidence still missing.

04 / Evidence

What the prototype showed—and what it didn’t.

The early result was useful as a signal about workflow potential. It was not enough to establish customer impact, production performance, or operating economics.

Prototype test · Real estate
10 → 3–5 min

Faster first-pass qualification

In prototype testing, the first-pass process moved from around 10 minutes to 3–5 minutes. This was an early test result—not a production KPI or sustained time-saving measure.

Still to validate

Live-user value and actual cost

The real-estate prototype was built for a client but not tested with live users. Token usage and paid-API costs also needed measurement before drawing conclusions about viability.

05 / Reflection

The next step was better evidence.

I would have invested more time and resources in validation before moving further. First, I’d test with paid APIs to measure token usage and the real cost of running the workflows. Then I’d bring the prototypes to users earlier, so product decisions were grounded in actual usage—not just what I could demonstrate in a prototype.

What I took forward: I can work from the business problem to a testable AI prototype—and use what I learn to make more grounded product decisions with design and engineering.
