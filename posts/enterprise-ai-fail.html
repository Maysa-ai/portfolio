Essay: AI & Product Strategy

Why enterprise AI projects fail before the model is even chosen

By the time anyone's comparing vendors, the project has usually already succeeded or failed.

Taking a regulated healthcare product through pilot and toward international rollout taught me something that has nothing to do with healthcare, and everything to do with why enterprise AI initiatives stall: the failure point is almost never the technology. It's the point, well before any technology gets chosen, where nobody agreed on what "working" actually means.

The real failure point is alignment, not architecture

On that project, patients, physiotherapists, medical stakeholders, the business side and Legal each had legitimate, competing priorities — and no shared framework to weigh them against. Left alone, every group will optimize for its own version of success. Enterprise AI has the identical structure: data and engineering want technical feasibility, the business wants ROI, compliance has constraints, and each group quietly assumes their definition of "success" is the one everyone's building toward. A model gets chosen, a pilot gets built, and months later someone discovers the project was solving three different problems at once. That's not a technology failure. Nobody agreed on the problem before the build started.

Constraints belong in the room from day one

Physiotherapist identity verification against official professional databases wasn't a legal footnote on that project — it was a core product requirement, built in from the start rather than bolted on after the fact. Enterprise AI projects routinely treat data governance, compliance and regulatory constraints the same wrong way: as a review gate near the end, instead of a design input from the beginning. A model selected without those constraints already shaping the requirements isn't a finished decision. It's a decision that's going to be redone.

A shared North Star beats a shared vendor

What actually fixed the alignment problem wasn't a meeting where everyone compromised. It was introducing a clear North Star and prioritization framework that patients, physiotherapists, medical stakeholders, business and Legal could all make decisions against — the same evidence, the same direction, before individual feature or technology decisions got made. That's the step enterprise AI projects skip. Everyone wants to get to the vendor bake-off. The organizations that succeed are the ones willing to slow down long enough to agree on what they're actually optimizing for first.

In a regulated environment, the constraint isn't a limitation on product thinking — it's part of the problem you're designing for.

The pattern, applied to enterprise AI

Same method, same order of operations:

Build

Get the real constraints — data, regulatory, stakeholder — on the table before any vendor conversation.

Align

Build one shared North Star everyone decides against — not five competing definitions of success.

Amplify

Pick the model last, not first — once the problem is fully specified.
