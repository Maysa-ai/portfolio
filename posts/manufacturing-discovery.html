Essay: Product Discovery

What building a manufacturing business taught me about product discovery

Everyone learns "discovery" as interviews and surveys inside a software team. I learned the real discipline of it somewhere else entirely — running a furniture factory.

My first startup was a luxury fashion social-commerce platform. I built the product, assembled a team, raised early interest from investors — and it closed without reaching sustainable revenue. I'd invested significant time and resources into a solution before ever proving the market needed it. That failure is where my real education in discovery began, years before I ever sat in a product management role.

Lesson one: the loudest signal isn't the real constraint

After that failure, I started again — deliberately smaller. I began selling furniture online through social channels in India, minimal inventory, testing demand before committing capital. It worked. Sales were healthy. And that's exactly when I almost missed the real problem.

The visible signal was sales, and sales were fine. The actual constraint was underneath it: margins were capped, quality depended entirely on third-party suppliers, and growth was limited by factors I didn't control. If I'd optimized what was visible — more marketing, more listings, more channels — I'd have been polishing a business model that couldn't scale. The real discovery wasn't "are people buying?" It was "why can't this grow past a certain point, no matter what I sell?"

Lesson two: validate at the smallest scale that tells you the truth

Once I understood the real constraint was the model itself — resale, not manufacturing — the temptation was to jump straight to a factory. Instead, I moved in stages: studying manufacturers and exporters in Rajasthan first, then a showroom and personalized consultation model to test demand for customized furniture, before committing to a ~3,000m² manufacturing operation in Jodhpur. Each step was sized to answer one question, not to look impressive.

That habit — proving something at the smallest scale that gives you a real answer, before scaling resources against it — is the same discipline good product discovery is built on. The instinct to build the full thing first is almost always wrong, whether you're sourcing furniture or shipping a feature.

Lesson three: build the feedback loop into the operation itself

The showroom wasn't just a sales channel — it was a research instrument. Talking to customers face-to-face about customization requests told me more about product-market fit than any dashboard could. Later, when I moved into manufacturing and had to manage suppliers, procurement and quality directly, I started introducing tighter operational controls specifically where partner management and raw-material transparency were creating risk — because the feedback loop needs to be built into how the business runs, not treated as a separate phase that happens before "real work" starts.

This is the piece I see software teams skip most often: discovery as an event, not a structure. A round of interviews before a sprint, then back to shipping. The businesses — and the products — that hold up are the ones where the feedback loop is load-bearing, not bolted on.

Sustainable growth rarely comes from treating symptoms. It comes from understanding the system deeply enough to identify where value is truly created, then having the courage to redesign it.

The pattern, named

I've come to think of this as a simple method I now apply to any product problem, in software or otherwise:

Build

Find the root of the problem, not the symptom sitting on top of it.

Align

Get people working from the same evidence, not trading opinions.

Amplify

Validate small, then scale — never the other way around.
