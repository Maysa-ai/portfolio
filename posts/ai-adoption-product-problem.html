Essay: AI & Product Strategy

Why AI adoption is a product problem, not just a technology problem

Most AI adoption efforts stall for reasons that have nothing to do with the model.

When I made the deliberate decision to go hands-on with AI — Python, LLMs, embeddings, RAG, automation — I expected the hard part to be technical. It wasn't. The hard part was everything a technology-first view of "AI adoption" skips entirely: which problem is actually worth solving, who needs to agree it's worth solving, and how you prove it works before you bet real resources on it. That's product work. The model is almost never where adoption actually fails.

The real question isn't which model — it's which problem

Working directly with clients on their day-to-day operations, the pattern that kept surfacing had nothing to do with AI capability. Lead qualification and processing consumed disproportionate time relative to the value it created. That's a workflow diagnosis, not a technology one — and it's the step most AI adoption efforts skip. Companies pick a model or a vendor first and go looking for a use case to justify it. The order should run the other way: find the workflow where the effort-to-value ratio is worst, then ask what would actually fix it. Sometimes that's AI. Often it's a smaller, less exciting fix that never gets considered because the mandate was "adopt AI," not "fix this."

Hands-on judgment beats secondhand judgment

I chose to learn the underlying stack myself — Python, data operations, embeddings, vector databases, RAG, MLOps — rather than stay on the strategy side and delegate the technical judgment to someone else. That decision changes what kind of product calls you're able to make. Assessing AI feasibility secondhand means trusting someone else's read on what's genuinely possible versus what's marketing. Assessing it firsthand means you can tell the difference between "this is hard" and "this is hard because we haven't scoped it correctly" — and that distinction is usually where AI initiatives either find real value or quietly stall.

Prove it narrow before you scale it wide

The instinct inside most companies is to announce an AI transformation program before a single workflow has been proven. I chose the opposite: I kept my own AI work at prototype and pilot stage with real clients, deliberately, rather than rush toward a polished commercial product. The goal wasn't caution for its own sake — it was building genuine judgment about where AI creates real value versus where it's hype, before committing anything bigger on top of it. A narrow, proven win is worth more than a wide, unproven mandate. Most adoption failures I've seen are the second thing dressed up as the first.

Understanding a technology in the abstract isn't the same as knowing where it actually creates value. I wanted to build that judgment myself, not borrow it.

The pattern, applied to AI

The same method holds here as everywhere else:

Build

Find the workflow bottleneck AI would actually fix — not the flashiest use case.

Align

Put technical judgment and business judgment in the same room — ideally the same person.

Amplify

Prove it narrow before you scale it wide.
